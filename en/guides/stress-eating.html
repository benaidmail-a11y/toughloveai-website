Stress Eating: Why It Happens and What Really Helps

Updated November 26, 2026 · ToughLove editorial team · about 5 min read

In short: Under ongoing stress, many people reach for sweet and fatty foods because they soothe for a moment and appeal to the reward system. If you spot your stress patterns, have other outlets and don’t blame yourself after a slip, you can break the cycle.

What happens in your body under stress

A sudden shock dampens appetite in many people at first. With ongoing stress it’s often the opposite: the stress hormone cortisol stays raised and the desire for energy-dense, sweet and fatty food grows. In a lab study, women who responded to stress with a lot of cortisol ate more afterwards, especially sweets (Epel et al., 2001). Eating also activates the brain’s reward system and brings brief relief (Adam & Epel, 2007). That’s why it so easily becomes a habit.

How to recognise stress eating

  • You eat although you aren’t really hungry.
  • The urge comes suddenly and wants something specific.
  • You eat fast, while doing something else or straight from the packet.
  • Afterwards you don’t feel better, but rather guilty.

One simple question helps: “Am I hungry, or am I tired, stressed, bored or lonely?”

Find your patterns

For one week, briefly note when you eat between meals: time, situation, feeling, what you ate. Patterns almost always show up, such as “after the call with my boss” or “on Sundays when I’m alone”. Once you know them, you can prepare instead of fighting yourself in the moment.

6 things that relieve stress instead of food

  1. Breathe for a minute. In for four seconds, hold for four, out for four, hold for four, a few rounds. It calms you and creates distance from the urge.
  2. Move briefly. Five minutes of fresh air or a trip up and down the stairs releases tension.
  3. Name the urge. “I’m stressed and I want a reward”, said out loud or written down. What you name steers you less.
  4. Eat regularly. If you’re stressed and hungry, you hardly stand a chance. Regular meals take one amplifier away from stress.
  5. Talk to someone. A message to a friend often helps more than a bar of chocolate.
  6. Be kind to yourself. Blaming yourself after a slip makes the next one more likely. In one study, participants who were helped to be self-compassionate ate less afterwards (Adams & Leary, 2007).

Frequently asked questions

Why do I eat so much when I’m stressed?

With ongoing stress, the hormone cortisol stays raised and the desire for sweet and fatty food grows. Eating soothes briefly via the reward system and easily becomes a habit.

What helps right away against stress eating?

Name the urge, breathe mindfully for a minute or step outside. That creates distance until the urge fades.

Is stress eating an eating disorder?

Occasional stress eating is common and not an eating disorder. But if you often lose control or suffer a lot from it, get medical or therapeutic support.
