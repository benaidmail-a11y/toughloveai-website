Changing Habits: How Long Does It Really Take?

Updated November 26, 2026 · ToughLove editorial team · about 5 min read

In short: The famous “21 days” is a myth. In one study, it took a median of 66 days for a new behaviour to become automatic, anywhere from 18 to 254 days depending on the person and the habit. One missed day barely sets you back. What matters is a fixed cue, a small start and a plan for hard moments.

Where the 21 days come from

The number goes back to observations from the 1960s that people get used to changes after about three weeks. It has little to do with building habits. If it still takes effort after three weeks, you haven’t failed: you’re simply in the middle of it.

What research actually shows

In a well-known study, 96 adults chose a new daily behaviour, such as a glass of water with lunch or a 15-minute walk after breakfast, and rated every day how automatic it felt. It took a median of 66 days to reach peak automaticity, with a huge range of 18 to 254 days (Lally et al., 2010). Simple habits like drinking water stuck faster than demanding ones like exercise.

There’s good news in the study too: missing one day had hardly any effect on later progress. You don’t need to be perfect; just carry on after a gap.

Why habits matter so much for weight loss

Motivation goes up and down. Habits run almost by themselves even on tired, stressful days, because they’re tied to situations rather than willpower. Experts therefore recommend deliberately turning health behaviour into habits: one specific action, repeated in the same situation (Gardner, Lally & Wardle, 2012).

How to build a new habit

  1. Start small. Choose “five minutes outside after dinner” over “an hour of exercise every day”. Small wins because it also works on bad days.
  2. Pick a fixed cue. Attach the new behaviour to something you already do: after brushing your teeth, arriving at work, after lunch.
  3. Make if-then plans. “If I turn on the TV in the evening, I’ll set a glass of water next to me.” Such plans clearly raise your chance of following through; a large review of 94 studies found a medium to large effect (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006).
  4. Plan the hard moment in advance. “If I want to hit the fridge after a stressful day, I’ll have a tea first and wait ten minutes.”
  5. Make it visible. Ticks on a calendar or a streak in an app make progress tangible, and carrying on easier.
  6. Get back right after a miss. Not “starting Monday”, but next time. One missed day is normal; two are the beginning of quitting.

Getting rid of old habits

Bad habits rarely disappear through banning alone. It works better to spot the cue and replace the behaviour. If you automatically eat crisps while watching series, stop keeping them within reach and hold something else: a cup of tea, vegetable sticks, some knitting. The cue stays, the response changes.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to change a habit?

In one study it took a median of 66 days for a new behaviour to become automatic. Depending on the person and habit, the range was 18 to 254 days.

Is it true that you form a habit in 21 days?

No. The 21 days are a myth. For most people it takes clearly longer, especially for demanding habits like exercise.

Is it bad if I miss a day?

No. In research, a single missed day had little effect on whether the habit formed. What matters is carrying on right away.
